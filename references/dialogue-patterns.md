# Dialogue patterns

Use these patterns to turn source analysis into a conversation that teaches through interaction.

## The learning loop

Build most concept clusters with this flexible sequence:

1. **Introduce:** Name the problem in ordinary language.
2. **Question:** Surface a plausible confusion, inference, or objection.
3. **Reason:** Work through the mechanism or evidence step by step.
4. **Test:** Apply the idea to a concrete example, boundary case, or counterexample.
5. **Rephrase:** Let the listening host restate the model in their own words, imperfectly when useful.
6. **Qualify:** Confirm what is right and repair what is incomplete.
7. **Extend:** Show why the idea changes the next question.

Do not force all seven moves into every section. Use enough to make the listener's mental-model change audible.

## Rotate expertise

Do not assign one permanent expert and one permanent novice. Give each host domains in which they lead, ask, challenge, and revise. A host can be knowledgeable while still acting as the listener proxy for a particular inference.

Useful host contrasts include:

- mechanism versus consequence
- caution versus experimentation
- individual experience versus system behavior
- theoretical cleanliness versus operational reality
- short-term utility versus long-term risk

Treat these as perspectives, not caricatures.

## Make questions do work

A useful question should perform at least one function:

- reveal missing knowledge
- test an inference
- request the reason behind a conclusion
- expose a boundary condition
- connect an abstract idea to a familiar scenario
- challenge whether the evidence supports the claimed implication

Replace questions that merely mean “please continue your lecture.”

Weak:

> HOST B: That's fascinating. What else did the researchers find?

Stronger:

> HOST B: If the system retrieves the right fact but uses it at the wrong moment, does the evaluation count that as a memory failure or a reasoning failure?

## Model reasoning, not just conclusions

Let listeners hear the intermediate steps:

- state the current assumption
- introduce the evidence or mechanism
- show what changes
- test the updated view
- retain the qualification

Avoid compressing every explanation into a polished verdict.

## Use disagreement honestly

Ground disagreement in different priorities, interpretations, or thresholds. Let hosts paraphrase each other's actual position before escalating it. Avoid reflexive opposition in every turn.

A credible disagreement may end with:

- changed belief
- partial agreement
- clearer terms but continued disagreement
- delayed resolution
- an explicit empirical question

Do not require an apology or comforting consensus. Correct material factual errors even when relationship tension remains unresolved.

## Keep chemistry functional

Use warmth, humor, callbacks, and short reactions only when they reveal attention or alter the conversation. Avoid:

- interchangeable enthusiasm
- constant name use
- scripted laughter
- fake personal anecdotes
- exaggerated teasing
- banter that delays the promised topic
- identical sentence lengths and turn sizes

Vary the rhythm. Give complex reasoning room to breathe, then use shorter turns for clarification or pressure-testing.

## Build transitions causally

Prefer transitions that expose a new problem:

- “That explains how it is stored, but not who gets to change it.”
- “The benchmark rewards recall. The user may care more about correction.”
- “Our example works for one agent; what breaks when several agents share the same record?”

Avoid announcing paper sections or saying “moving on” unless the format specifically calls for it.

## Write a purposeful ending

Return to the episode promise with a changed understanding. Use a concrete distinction, decision rule, image, or unresolved question. End while the final idea still has energy.

Avoid generic endings such as “technology has benefits and risks” or a complete recap of points the listener just heard.
